# How to Vet a Property Developer in Cyprus: Delivery Record, Title Deeds and Warning Signs

*Last updated: [DATE] · By Sascha Dith, CEO, Cyprus VIP Estates*

Most buying guides tell you how to check a property. Far fewer tell you how to check the company selling it — and in Cyprus, buying new-build off-plan, that is the more consequential question.

You are handing over money for something that may not exist yet, on land you do not yet own, on the strength of a company's promise to finish it and hand you the deeds. The building can be exactly as advertised and you can still end up in difficulty if the developer's finances are not.

This guide is about judging the company. The legal checks on the property itself — the title search, the encumbrance report, the contract terms — belong to your lawyer, and we cover the mechanics separately in [what a title deed is in Cyprus](/blog/what-is-a-title-deed) and [the ten mistakes first-time buyers make](/blog/top-10-mistakes-first-time-buyers).

## Why Choosing the Right Developer in Cyprus Matters More Than the Building

Cyprus has a specific, well-documented problem that makes developer selection unusually important: the **trapped buyer**.

It works like this. A developer borrows against the land to fund construction, and the bank registers a charge over that land. You buy a unit, pay in full, move in — and the Land Registry still cannot issue you an individual title deed, because the developer's mortgage sits over the whole plot. Until that charge is released, the deed cannot be split out and transferred to you.

The property is yours in every practical sense and not yours on paper. You cannot mortgage it, and selling it in the normal way becomes difficult.

Remedial legislation exists and has helped many buyers, but those cases take years to resolve. The far cheaper protection is choosing a developer where the situation never arises.

## The Title Deed Test: Has the Developer Issued Deeds on Past Cyprus Projects?

If you ask one question, ask this one.

Not "will I get the title deed" — every developer says yes. Ask: **on your last three completed projects, have individual title deeds been issued to buyers, and how long did it take from handover?**

A developer with a clean record will answer specifically and can point you to completed developments where owners hold their deeds. A developer with a problem will answer in generalities, blame the Land Registry, or explain that it is "in process" — a phrase that can describe six months or six years.

Your lawyer can verify the answer. Deeds are public record at the Department of Lands and Surveys, so a claim about a past project is checkable rather than a matter of trust.

This one question tells you more than any brochure, because it measures the outcome you actually care about rather than the intention.

## How to Check a Cyprus Developer's Completed Projects and Delivery Record

**Completed projects, not planned ones.** A developer's website leads with what is coming. What matters is what has been finished. Ask for a list of completed developments with dates, and check that the number of completions is consistent with how long the company says it has been operating.

**Delivery against the original date.** Off-plan contracts carry a completion date. Ask whether recent projects met theirs, and by how much they slipped if not. Delays happen in construction everywhere; a pattern of two-year overruns is different from a three-month one.

**How long the company has existed, and under what name.** Construction companies sometimes restructure after difficulty and continue under a new entity. Your lawyer can check company registration and confirm the trading history is what it appears to be.

**Who built it, physically.** Some developers subcontract everything and coordinate; others have their own construction teams. Neither is wrong, but it changes who is accountable if there is a defect after handover.

## Mortgages and Charges on the Land: What the Land Registry Search Shows

This is the question underneath the title-deed question, and it is answerable before you commit.

Your lawyer requests a **search certificate** from the Land Registry for the plot the development sits on. It shows the registered owner, and critically, any **encumbrances** — mortgages, charges, liens. If the developer has borrowed against the land, it will be recorded there.

A charge on the land is not automatically a reason to walk away. Most developers finance construction somehow, and many discharge those charges cleanly on completion. What matters is whether the contract obliges the developer to release the charge over your unit, and whether they have done so reliably before.

What should worry you is a developer who is evasive about it, or a contract that says nothing on the subject.

## Depositing the Contract at the Land Registry: Your Specific Performance Right

There is one legal step that protects you even if things go wrong later, and it is time-limited.

When you sign the sale contract, it must be **deposited at the Land Registry**, within **six months** of signing. Doing so creates a **Specific Performance Right** — your right to have the property registered in your name, enforceable even if the developer subsequently runs into financial trouble or tries to sell the same unit twice.

Miss that window and you lose that protection. It is a formality, it is your lawyer's job, and it is the single most valuable thing that happens in the entire transaction. Confirm it has been done rather than assuming.

## Questions to Ask a Cyprus Developer Before Paying a Reservation Fee

Ask these directly. How a developer answers is itself information.

- On your last three completed projects, have title deeds been issued to buyers, and how long did that take?
- Is there a mortgage or charge registered over this land? What does the contract say about releasing it?
- What is the contractual completion date, and what happens if it is missed? Is there a penalty clause?
- What is the payment schedule tied to — calendar dates or construction milestones?
- Which completed developments can I visit?
- Do you hold the building permit and, for finished projects, the certificate of final approval?
- Who handles construction — your own teams or subcontractors?

A confident developer with a clean record finds these questions unremarkable. Defensiveness is a signal.

## Red Flags When Buying Off-Plan Property From a Cyprus Developer

**Pressure to pay a reservation fee before your lawyer has seen anything.** Reservation fees are normal; urgency around them is not.

**Payments tied to dates rather than construction stages.** Milestone-based payment means you pay for progress that has actually happened. Calendar-based payment means you pay regardless.

**Being steered towards a specific lawyer.** Your lawyer must be independent of the seller. A developer recommending "their" lawyer is not necessarily acting badly, but you should not accept the recommendation.

**Vagueness about title deeds on past projects.** See above. This is the test.

**A price notably below comparable projects nearby.** Occasionally this reflects a genuine early-stage discount. More often it reflects something about the land, the permits or the finances.

**Reluctance to show you completed work.** A developer with finished buildings wants you to see them.

## Visiting Completed Developments in Cyprus: What to Look For

If you can visit, look at a development finished three to five years ago rather than one handed over last month. New buildings look good. The question is how they age.

Check the common areas, the state of the pool and landscaping, and whether the lifts and gates work. Talk to residents if you can — owners are candid about their developer, and one conversation in a courtyard is worth more than a brochure.

If you are buying remotely and cannot visit, ask your agent or lawyer to visit on your behalf and send photographs of a completed project, not a show unit.

## Developer Due Diligence: What Your Lawyer Checks and What Only You Can Judge

**Your lawyer's job:** the title search and encumbrance report, the building permit and planning compliance, the contract terms, depositing the contract at the Land Registry, and confirming taxes and municipal fees on the property are paid.

**Your job:** whether the company answers questions straight, whether its completed work looks like work you want to own, whether the payment schedule is tied to progress, and whether you were rushed.

The legal checks catch what is documented. They do not tell you whether a company is one you want to depend on for the two years it takes to build your home. That judgement stays with you.
